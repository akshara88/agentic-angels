This idea fits the specialist-swarm pattern naturally — unlike the investment pipeline before, these six lenses (founder, market, product, financial, competitive, risk) are genuinely independent domains that can run in parallel with no sequencing dependency between them, which is the right structure for a swarm rather than a pipeline. Let me brainstorm the edge cases first, then build the architecture.
Brainstorm: what makes this hard
Where the six specialists naturally conflict (and that's a feature, not a bug):
Founder specialist loves a first-time founder's vision; Risk specialist flags first-time-founder execution risk as a red flag. These should disagree — a committee's value is surfacing that tension, not averaging it into a bland middle score.
Market specialist says TAM is huge; Competitive specialist says the market is already crowded with 6 well-funded players. Same market, opposite implications — needs to reach the synthesis stage as an explicit tension, not get smoothed over.
Product specialist is impressed by the demo; Financial specialist notices burn rate implies 4 months of runway. A great product with an imminent cash crisis is a specific, nameable risk pattern — the architecture needs to preserve it as a flagged conflict, not bury it in an averaged score.
Data availability varies wildly by specialist:
Financial and Competitive specialists can lean on public data, comparable company filings, market reports.
Founder and Product specialists mostly depend on what the founders themselves present (pitch deck, demo, references) — much higher risk of one-sided or curated input, so these two need an explicit "unverified / founder-provided" tag on their findings.
Risk specialist is different in kind from the other five — it's not gathering new evidence, it's actively looking for holes and contradictions across the other five specialists' outputs. That makes it a poor fit for pure parallel execution — it likely needs to run after the other five, seeing their outputs, which breaks pure symmetry in an otherwise clean swarm.
Committee dynamics (the synthesis problem):
With 6 independent voices, a naive "average the scores" approach hides exactly the disagreements a real investment committee argues about. The synthesis stage needs to surface disagreement, not resolve it silently.
Different VCs weight these six lenses differently (a seed-stage fund cares more about founder + market; a growth-stage fund cares more about financial + competitive). The weighting shouldn't be hardcoded — it should be a configurable input the committee chair applies.
ArchitectureThe five due-diligence specialists (purple) run genuinely in parallel — no ordering dependency between founder, market, product, financial, and competitive analysis. Risk (coral) sits in its own stage because it's structurally different from the other five: it's not gathering new evidence, it's actively looking for contradictions across their outputs, so it has to see all five results before it can do its job. Synthesis (teal) is the committee chair — it doesn't average the six inputs into one score, it applies whatever weighting the VC's stage/thesis calls for and explicitly surfaces disagreements rather than smoothing them into a bland consensus.
A few design choices worth flagging, since they matter more than the boxes themselves:
The weighting logic in synthesis should be a configurable input, not hardcoded — a seed fund and a growth fund would want the same five specialist outputs weighted very differently.
Founder and Product specialists should tag their findings as "founder-provided/unverified" where relevant, since their main data source is what the startup itself presents — that's a materially different confidence level than Financial or Competitive, which can lean on public/third-party data.
Every output should carry a research date — funding rounds, pivots, and shutdowns happen fast enough that a memo without a timestamp is misleading a week later.
